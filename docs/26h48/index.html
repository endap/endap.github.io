  • The magnetization of iron cannon barrels by the Earth’s magnetic field, followed by scanning with a magnetic compass over the length of the barrel, might be the first documented use of the magnetic flux leakage concept for finding cracks or defects in a metal structure (Saxby, 1868). 1
  • Later, it was observed that metallic grinding particles did not distribute randomly on a steel component magnetized by a magnetic chuck; instead, they formed visible patterns corresponding to cracks or discontinuities on the steel surface (Hoke, 1922). 2.
  • In the 1930s, Magnaflux Corporation helped commercialize magnetic particle testing and spread its use in industry after de Forest and Doane developed it into a practical inspection method (Betz, 1967). 3
  • During the 1960s, the non-destructive testing (NDT) industry transitioned from visual observation and manual magnetic particle testing to automated Magnetic Flux Leakage (MFL) by integrating electromagnetic sensors (such as induction coils and early Hall-effect sensors) into internal pipeline inspection gauges (“smart pigs”), such as
    • how stray magnetic leakage fields generated by surface and near-surface defects could be captured electronically using micro-coils and probe arrays, proving that electronic sensors could reliably replace human visual interpretation of magnetic particle accumulation (Förster, 1962), 4
    • replacement of optical/photographic crawlers with onboard magnetizing yokes and solid-state electromagnetic sensors to record continuous voltage variations onto internal magnetic tape as the pig traveled through the pipe (Allen & Wood, 1966), 5
    • the operational decision to abandon visual/camera-based internal inspection devices due to lighting and cleanliness limitations, in favor of dedicated MFL smart pigs capable of quantitatively mapping metal loss across entire national transmission grids (Clerehugh & Knowles, 1975). 6
  • The use of MFL is changed from previously only to detect the presence of a flaw, then it becomes to characterize the defect like depth, width, and shape; and separate true material defect signal from background magnetic noise and surface irregularities (Bainton, 1977). 7
  • Using multiple sensors with certain configurations and magnetic concentrators can enhanced the reading in different axes, which is very important for steel rope (Kalwa & Piekarski, 1987). 8
